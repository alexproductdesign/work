Turning an invisible account into a product people actually use

Trusted Shops is a European platform that helps online stores build trust through trustmarks, verified reviews and Buyer Protection. The Trusted Account was meant to be the central touchpoint for consumers – managing protection, writing reviews, interacting with shops. In reality, most users didn't even know they had one.

Problem

Strong B2B adoption, invisible B2C product

Despite strong B2B adoption, the consumer account was underused. Only ~12% of users returned after first login, profile completion sat under 6%, and fewer than 1% upgraded to the PLUS subscription. Many users didn't realize they had an account at all – including people already paying for PLUS.

We were selling security, and security was nowhere to be seen in the product.

Research

Six sources of evidence before a single screen

There was no fresh research, so I built the picture from six inputs: two new studies I ran, and four sources the company already had but nobody had put together.

📝 User surveys

Sent right after people paid for the PLUS subscription. Many didn't know they had an account, most couldn't name a single PLUS benefit. No understood purpose → no reason to come back.

🗣️ 1:1 interviews

Open, story-based conversations about buying online safely: goals, needs, pain points, and the words people actually use when they talk about risk.

📨 Support tickets

Zendesk tickets showed the same confusion on repeat: how Buyer Protection works, what PLUS includes, and how to report a problem when an order goes wrong.

📊 Product analytics

Only ~12% of users returned after first login. Profile completion under 6%. Subscription and review conversion each below 1% – reviews happened in emails, not in the account.

🤝 Internal interviews

Stakeholder conversations surfaced the real constraints: legal and brand limits on wording, questions around public-profile visibility, and a claim flow owned by another team.

🧭 Heuristic review

Against competitors, key actions were harder to discover and flows harder to follow – especially on mobile. Competitors solved the same tasks in fewer steps.

Method

The framework behind every study I run

I own B2C research here, so every study runs on the same framework – that's what makes results comparable between projects. Interview with open, story-based questions. Debrief the same day, one board per participant. Tag by goals, needs, motivations and pain points, then bring everyone onto one board. Cluster what different people said the same way and count it – the count is what turns a pile of notes into a finding. And a finding only becomes an insight when I can name the why and the consequence.

Audience

Two audiences, one attitude to risk

The survey told us who our audience actually is; the interviews explained why they behave that way. Two segments carry this product.

70% · Segment A · 55+

Retirees who shop online carefully. They read the shop page before the product page. The fear is concrete: the money is gone and nobody on the other side is dealing with it.

20% · Segment B · 40–55

Family buyers, mostly women, ordering for a household. High order volume, and they are the one who sorts it out when a parcel never arrives.

They did not grow up with the internet. Telling a trustworthy shop from a fake one is not obvious to them – and many have already been cheated online at least once.

Reframe

What we actually sell is peace of mind

Digital security isn't a feature you evaluate on a spec sheet. You buy it like insurance: emotionally, in advance, because the alternative is a feeling you don't want to have. Written as a ladder, in the customer's language:

Goal

I want to shop online without the fear of losing my money. → Drives the headline and the offer.

Needs

Know this order is covered. Know the shop is real. Know how to report a problem and what happens after that. → Drives the sections and the features.

Pains

No visible protection status, legal jargon, a hidden claim route, no idea what PLUS includes. → Drives what the redesign has to relieve.

The brief

From the brief to a content priority

How might we make the account visible, understandable and useful – so people see they are protected and act on it?

I mapped the full account journey from first entry after purchase through subscription and claims: the research pains turned out to be the structural blockers too. Once the priorities were questions instead of features, the team stopped arguing about taste and started arguing about order of work.

Priority

🧾 Detailed order overview – what did I buy, when, and is it protected?

Priority

🛡️ Protection status – am I covered, and until when?

Priority

📄 Open protection claims – clear steps, easy to find, no legal jargon.

Priority

💳 Subscription status – do I have PLUS, what's included, how to manage?

Priority

✍️ Review prompts – can I leave a review here, and why should I?

Success metrics

Six metrics, agreed with the PM before anything was drawn

Tracked in GA, Metabase and backend event logs – later Mixpanel, Looker, BigQuery, Zendesk and Hotjar.

Retention

Retention rate – users returning to the app after their first session. The headline metric, and an output rather than something we can act on directly.

Revenue

Subscription conversion – free to paid via the account.

Engagement

Verified public profile completion · monthly review contribution.

Support cost

Tickets for opening a protection case · tickets for understanding the subscription.

A target is a forecast, not a wish. For each metric we took the web version of the product as the baseline, estimated a deliberately conservative improvement from our own past changes and industry benchmarks – target = baseline × (1 + improvement %) – and paired every estimate with a plan to check it, measured the same way before and after launch.

Trade-offs

Three trade-offs we made to launch

Every launch is a set of deliberate compromises. Reading them before the results makes the results honest.

Fix the account, not the path to it

Checkout, emails, login and registration, and external entry points all belong to other teams. We gained a launch with no cross-team dependencies. It cost us this: people buy PLUS without understanding what they bought – and the one target we missed sits exactly there.

One big redesign, not a series of A/B tests

A coherent product and one clear release – all a team this size could run. The cost is attribution: every result below is the effect of the whole package, not of a single change.

Cut order tracking

The concept was tested and the final design was done – blocked by technical limitations, not by priority. The launch date held, but the account still can't answer "where is my parcel", the most frequent question of the family segment.

The tracking design that never shipped: delivery status inside the order, and the carrier timeline behind it.

Design

From workshops and wireframes to a gradual launch

I started with cross-functional workshops – product, support, legal and management – to align on goals and surface edge cases early. From there: user journeys and entry points for protection, profiles, subscriptions, reviews and orders; low-fi wireframes; quick prototypes to test flows like submitting a claim or upgrading to PLUS. Before launch we ran usability tests on the main flows.

For the final designs I worked closely with engineers, the PM and content design. We reused design-system components, added scalable patterns like protection badges and profile cards, and tested for accessibility and responsiveness with QA – which matters more than usual when a large share of your audience is 55+. We launched gradually – German mobile users first, then the other markets.

Outcome

Five of six targets met

We launched the redesigned account in December 2024 and started tracking results right away. Retention grew by 11 p.p., verified profile completion jumped 63 p.p., subscription conversion rose 1.8 p.p., and support tickets about opening a Buyer Protection case dropped 24 p.p.

Not everything hit target – reviews inside the account grew only 6 p.p. and subscription-related tickets fell just 10 p.p. People who met PLUS inside the account understood it; people who met it in a checkout or an email often didn't, and cancelled within days. That's a value-communication problem before the account – and the next iteration.

This work mixed research with teamwork and led to real results. It also pushed me into a more strategic role – where design choices shape both the user experience and the business.
